Fees & margin

Amazon raised fees twice in 2026. Most brands repriced for neither.

Two increases landed in the same year, seven weeks apart in effect. Neither was large on its own. Together they quietly moved the break-even point on every unit you ship, and almost nobody adjusted their price.

What actually changed

Two separate things, and they compound.

January 2026. Amazon adjusted its US fulfilment fees. The headline figure was small, an average of about eight cents a unit, which is why it was widely reported as a non-event. But the increase was not spread evenly. Smaller items priced above $50 took roughly an extra $0.51 per unit. If you sell a compact, higher-priced product, you were not in the eight-cent group.

April 2026. Amazon added a fuel and logistics surcharge of around 3.5%, applied on top of fulfilment fees. This one matters more than it sounds, because it is a percentage rather than a flat amount. It scales with your fee, which means it hits heavier and bulkier products hardest, and it applies on top of the January change rather than instead of it.

The combination is what catches people. A brand that checked the January announcement, decided eight cents was noise, and moved on, never revisited the question in April.

The maths on a real product

Take a product at a $52 selling price, shipping roughly 2,000 units a month. Run the two changes through it.

LinePer unitPer month
January fulfilment change, small item above $50$0.51$1,020
April surcharge, 3.5% on a $6.50 fulfilment fee$0.23$460
Combined$0.74$1,480

That is roughly $17,760 a year on a single product line, taken entirely out of contribution margin, with no change in what the customer pays and no change in what you buy the goods for.

Now apply the same arithmetic across a catalogue of thirty or forty products. The number stops being a rounding error.

Worth checking yourself

Fee schedules change and the figures above are the ones that applied to US fulfilment in 2026. Before you act on any of it, pull your own fee preview report in Seller Central and compare a unit today against the same unit in December. That report is the only version of this that is actually about your products.

The change nobody noticed

A third change went almost unmentioned, and for some brands it costs more than the other two combined.

The aged inventory surcharge now begins at 181 days rather than the 271 days it previously started at. Three months earlier. If you hold seasonal stock, or you buy in large container quantities to get a better unit cost, or you carry slow-moving sizes and colours to keep a variation family complete, that stock now starts accruing a surcharge a full quarter sooner than it used to.

The brands most exposed to this are the ones doing something sensible: buying in bulk to protect margin, or keeping a full size range so the listing does not look thin. Both behaviours now cost more than they did.

Why most brands missed it

Three reasons, and none of them are carelessness.

  • The headline number was small. Eight cents does not trigger a pricing review. The $0.51 band inside it did not make the headlines.
  • The two changes were months apart. A brand that assessed January had no reason to reassess in April, and the April change was a percentage buried in the fee schedule rather than an announcement.
  • Fee increases do not show up as a problem. They show up as a slightly worse month. Revenue looks the same. Units look the same. Advertising looks the same. The only line that moved is one most brands do not calculate per unit, because they track cost of sale against revenue rather than contribution after every Amazon charge.

That last point is the real one. If you set advertising targets on cost of sale rather than on contribution per unit, a fee increase is completely invisible to you. Your ACoS target does not know that the fee changed, so it keeps approving spend at a level that no longer works.

What to do about it

  1. Pull the fee preview report and compare a unit today with the same unit in December. Do it for your top ten products by volume, not the whole catalogue.
  2. Work out contribution per unit, not cost of sale. Landed cost, referral fee, fulfilment fee, surcharges, storage, and an allowance for returns. What is left is the number every advertising decision should be measured against.
  3. Set a break-even cost of sale per product from that contribution figure. A single account-wide ACoS target will always be wrong for most of your catalogue.
  4. Look hardest at products between $45 and $60. The $50 threshold means a small price change can move a product across a fee band in either direction.
  5. Review anything sitting past 150 days before it crosses the 181-day line. Removal, a markdown, or an advertising push all cost less than the surcharge plus another quarter of storage.

None of this requires new software. It requires the fee report, a spreadsheet, and an afternoon. The brands that will feel 2027 hardest are the ones still running 2025 pricing on 2026 fees.

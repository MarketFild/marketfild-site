A falling TACoS is not always good news

Total advertising cost of sale falling is one of the few metrics everyone agrees is good. It is not always. There are two completely different reasons it drops, and they lead to opposite futures.

Two reasons, two outcomes

TACoS is advertising spend divided by total revenue. It can fall in exactly two ways: revenue goes up, or spend goes down.

The good version. Your organic ranking improves, repeat purchases build, and revenue grows faster than advertising. Spend holds steady, the denominator gets bigger, TACoS falls. This is what everyone has in mind when they celebrate it, and it is a genuine achievement.

The other version. You cut advertising. Revenue holds up for a while, because the customers you acquired in previous months are still buying, and because organic ranking takes time to decay. TACoS falls sharply. On a dashboard the two look identical.

The difference is that the first version is a foundation and the second is a withdrawal. One compounds. The other runs out.

How to tell which one you have

Three numbers separate them, and you can pull all three in about twenty minutes.

1. New-to-brand customers

This is the one that matters most. Sponsored Brands campaigns report new-to-brand orders directly. Brand Analytics reports new versus returning customers at brand level. If TACoS is falling and new-to-brand is falling with it, you are not getting more efficient. You are buying fewer customers.

2. Organic revenue as a share of total

If organic share is rising because organic revenue is growing in absolute terms, that is real. If organic share is rising only because advertising revenue collapsed while organic stayed flat, the share went up and the business went down. Always look at the absolute number, not the percentage.

3. Cost per new customer

Divide total ad spend by new-to-brand customers. If that figure stayed roughly flat while spend halved, acquisition never got more expensive. You simply bought less of it. That is a budget decision, not an efficiency gain, and it should be made deliberately rather than discovered afterwards.

The distinction in one line

Efficiency means the same result for less money. A spend cut means less money and, eventually, less result. TACoS cannot tell them apart. Cost per new customer can.

The lag that hides it

The reason this goes unnoticed for months is that the damage arrives late.

A customer acquired in March does not repeat in March. Depending on your category and pack size, they repeat in June, or September, or never. For a consumable product on a roughly three-month replenishment cycle, the customers you fail to acquire this quarter are the repeat revenue you do not have next quarter.

So the sequence looks like this. You cut spend. TACoS improves immediately and everyone is pleased. Revenue holds for two months, because the previous cohorts are still buying. Then in the third month revenue starts sliding, and by then the cause is a quarter behind you and nobody connects the two.

It is the same reason cutting acquisition always looks free at first. The bill arrives after the decision has been forgotten.

Four checks worth running

  1. Plot new-to-brand customers next to ad spend for the last twelve months. If they move together, your advertising is doing acquisition work and cutting it has a cost.
  2. Plot repeat customers over the same period. Repeat usually correlates weakly with ad spend. If repeat is flat while new is collapsing, your revenue is currently being held up by past acquisition.
  3. Check organic revenue in absolute terms, not as a percentage of total.
  4. Check whether your highest cost-of-sale campaigns are your acquisition campaigns. Sponsored Brands video frequently runs at a cost of sale that looks poor on last-click, while delivering the overwhelming majority of new customers. Judging it on ACoS and pausing it is one of the most expensive mistakes available in an Amazon account.

A falling TACoS earned by growth is worth protecting. A falling TACoS bought by cutting acquisition is a loan against next quarter. They deserve very different reactions, and the metric itself will never tell you which one you are looking at.

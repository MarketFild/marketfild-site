Your multipack is better value and looks twice the price

Shifting customers to bigger packs is usually the right move. It also changes the number a shopper sees on the results page, and on broad searches that number is the whole decision.

What happens when the pack mix shifts

A brand decides to push the larger pack. Better order value, better replenishment fit, better margin after fulfilment. Over a year the mix moves from mostly small packs to mostly large ones. Every internal metric improves.

Meanwhile the average price a shopper sees has gone from around twelve dollars to around seventeen, because the pack got bigger. The price per unit inside the pack actually went down. Both facts are true at the same time, and only one of them is visible on the search results page.

What movedBeforeAfterChange
Average pack price shown$11.80$17.50+48%
Price per unit inside the pack$0.61$0.58−4%

Nobody raised a price. The brand got cheaper per unit and looked 48% more expensive. That is a mix effect, and it is invisible unless you deliberately look for it.

You are selling into two different markets

Here is the part that decides what to do about it.

Split your non-branded searches into two groups. One group names the attribute you are built for: organic, stainless, heavy duty, for sensitive skin, whatever your category's premium qualifier is. The other group is the plain generic term for the product.

These two behave nothing alike. In the qualified group, shoppers arrive expecting a premium price, and your pack may sit comfortably at or below the going rate. In the generic group, shoppers carry a reference price anchored to the cheapest thing on the page, and your large pack lands well above it.

The generic group is usually five to ten times larger in search volume. The qualified group usually delivers most of the purchases. Brands routinely discover that the majority of their advertising budget sits on the larger, weaker half, at a worse conversion rate, showing the pack that looks most expensive there.

The check

Take your non-branded search terms, tag each one as generic or qualified, and total spend, conversion and cost of sale for each group. If the generic half is taking more budget at a materially worse conversion rate, you have found a reallocation worth more than most listing work.

Make Amazon show the per-unit price

There is a mechanical fix that costs nothing and most brands never apply.

Where a listing carries a proper size variation with the unit count populated, Amazon will render a per-unit price next to each size option automatically. A shopper comparing your 18-count against your 54-count sees not only the pack prices but also the price per item, which is the comparison that favours you.

If your parent-child family exists but the size attribute or the unit count is not filled in, none of that appears. The shopper sees two prices, one of which looks large, and no explanation. This is a listing field, not a restructure, and it is usually an afternoon of work.

Two things to check while you are in there:

  • Is the whole family actually one listing? A split variation family divides your reviews and your ranking across listings that should be one.
  • Are the sizes in a sensible order? Shoppers anchor on the first option they see.

Match the pack to the query

The advertising change follows directly from the price analysis.

On generic searches, where the reference price is low, advertise the pack that meets that expectation. On qualified searches, where the shopper is already primed for a premium product, advertise the larger pack and let the per-unit price do the work.

Most accounts do the opposite by accident, because the large pack is the one everyone wants to sell, so it ends up behind the highest-volume terms. The result is the most expensive traffic in the account pointed at the product with the widest gap to the page's reference price. It earns the impression and loses the click.

Move a portion, not all of it. Hold the rest for four weeks and read the result on new-to-brand orders as well as cost of sale, because the qualified pool is smaller and will saturate. Cutting generic reach too hard is its own mistake, and it is slower to notice.

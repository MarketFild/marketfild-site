Quick commerce

The first 90 days on Blinkit, Zepto and Instamart

Brands that treat quick commerce as another marketplace listing exercise usually lose money on it. The buyer is different, the basket is different, and the thing that gets you delisted is different.

Why it is not a marketplace

On a marketplace, a shopper searches, compares, reads reviews and often decides later. On quick commerce they are deciding in seconds from a short list, frequently on a phone, frequently replenishing something they have bought before.

That changes almost everything downstream.

  • Smaller packs, bought more often. Your marketplace hero product is often the wrong lead here.
  • Visibility is negotiated, not earned. There is no slow climb up an organic ranking. Placement is commercial.
  • A stockout is not a ranking problem, it is a relationship problem. On a marketplace you lose position. Here you get a conversation about delisting.
  • Distribution is city by city and store by store, not a national warehouse.

Commercials come before catalogue

Getting listed is the easy part. Getting listed on terms you can live with for the next year is the work.

Before anything else is agreed, you want clarity on margin structure, listing and slotting fees, return and damage treatment, payment cycles, and what visibility costs. These are set early and are difficult to renegotiate once volume exists, because your leverage is highest before you are dependent.

Model the unit economics at the proposed terms before you sign, not after the first month's statement. A product that works at marketplace margins can lose money here once platform margin and visibility spend are both in the calculation.

Assortment and pack architecture

The single most common mistake is listing the existing catalogue unchanged.

Quick commerce rewards a tight range of high-frequency, impulse-friendly packs at a price point that suits an unplanned purchase. A large value pack that performs well on a marketplace often sits still here, occupying shelf space and dragging your per-store velocity down, which is the number the platform judges you on.

Three questions per product before it goes on:

  1. Would someone buy this without planning to?
  2. Is the price point inside the range people spend without thinking?
  3. Does it sell often enough to justify the shelf slot it takes?

If the answer to any of those is no, keep it off. A short, fast-moving range beats a complete one.

Cities, not coverage

Where you launch decides sell-through more than your creative does.

Spreading thinly across every city in a metro produces low velocity everywhere, which reads to the platform as weak demand. Concentrating on the clusters where your category actually indexes produces strong numbers in a smaller footprint, which is what earns you expansion.

Start narrow, prove velocity, then expand on the strength of it. It feels slower and it is considerably faster.

Fill rate is the whole game

On a marketplace, a forecasting error shows up next month as lost sales. Here it shows up the same week, as either a stockout or as wastage, and both are visible to the platform.

Fill rate is the metric the platform uses to decide how much of you it wants. Hold weekly numbers, agree replenishment triggers rather than reacting to them, and move before a gap becomes a conversation. Brands that are consistently available get offered growth. Brands that are not get quietly reduced.

The honest version

Quick commerce rewards operational discipline more than marketing skill. If your supply chain cannot reliably restock a dark store cluster every week, the marketing plan does not matter yet.

What usually goes wrong

  • The marketplace catalogue is copied across unchanged, including pack sizes built for a considered purchase.
  • Launch is too wide, producing weak velocity everywhere and no case for expansion.
  • Commercials are agreed quickly to get live, and become the ceiling on profitability for a year.
  • Visibility spend is spread evenly through the month rather than concentrated on the days the category actually converts.
  • Nobody owns fill rate, so it is only discussed after a delisting warning.
  • It is bolted onto a marketplace retainer and run by someone whose day is already full, which is how most of the above happens.

Done properly, quick commerce is a genuinely different discipline with its own weekly rhythm. Treated as a side listing exercise, it is an expensive way to discover that.

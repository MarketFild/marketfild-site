Retention

Your repeat customer percentage went up. That might be the problem.

Retention percentages are a ratio, and a ratio can move because the top changed or because the bottom changed. When the bottom is new customers, a rising retention rate can be the clearest warning sign in the account.

The arithmetic

Repeat customer share is repeat customers divided by total customers. Total customers is new plus repeat. So the share rises in either of two situations:

  • Repeat customers increase, which is genuinely good
  • New customers decrease, which is genuinely bad

Both produce the same upward line on the same chart. If you only report the percentage, the two are indistinguishable, and one of them will be read as a success when it is the opposite.

A worked example

Take a brand over three months. Repeat customers barely move. New customers fall by nearly two-thirds.

MonthNewRepeatTotalRepeat %
June2,5001,5404,04038.1%
July1,2101,4902,70055.2%
August8801,5402,42063.6%

Repeat share climbed from 38% to 64% in two months. On a slide, that is a retention story worth presenting. In the data, repeat customers are exactly where they started, and the business lost more than 1,600 new customers a month.

The percentage did not rise because anything improved. It rose because the denominator fell out.

The rule

Never report a retention percentage without the absolute new-customer count beside it. One number without the other is not a metric, it is a shape.

Why it matters more than it looks

Two reasons this particular illusion is expensive.

It arrives at the same time as the other reassuring numbers. A drop in new customers usually comes from a drop in traffic, which usually comes from a cut in advertising. That same cut improves your cost of sale and your total advertising cost of sale. So you get three metrics moving in the "right" direction at once, all caused by the same underlying problem. The account has never looked healthier on a dashboard and never been in more trouble.

It delays the reaction by a full cycle. Repeat revenue is the most stable part of most accounts, so it holds the total up while acquisition falls away underneath. By the time total revenue drops, the cause is one or two quarters behind you.

There is also a definition trap worth knowing about. Amazon reports repeat behaviour in more than one way. One measure counts customers who ordered more than once within a single month. Another counts customers who had ever bought from the brand before. The same account can be "10% repeat" and "60% repeat" in the same month, both correct, measuring different things. If your reporting quietly switches between them, the trend is meaningless.

What to track instead

  1. New-to-brand customers, as a count. Monthly, absolute, never as a share. This is the health of your acquisition.
  2. Repeat customers, as a count. Also absolute. This is the health of your product and your replenishment.
  3. Cost per new customer. Ad spend divided by new-to-brand customers. Tells you whether acquisition is getting harder or just getting smaller.
  4. Your replenishment interval. Roughly how many months pass between a customer's orders. It tells you how far ahead of a revenue problem your acquisition numbers are warning you.

Keep the percentage if you like it. Just never let it travel without the two counts underneath it, and label which definition of repeat you are using every single time.
